# Historical strategies and explanations

Use when outcomes already occurred and intervention is unavailable. Analyze mechanisms and competing explanations; do not report retrospective support as randomized causal proof.

1. Define the case population and selection rule before selecting success stories. Include relevant failures, abandoned cases and counterexamples.
2. Specify what each explanation predicts about intermediate events, timing, and boundary conditions. Seek evidence that distinguishes explanations, not just evidence compatible with the preferred one.
3. Preserve source, publication date, decision-time availability, and exact observation. Separate contemporary records from later recollection and the researcher's interpretation.
4. Trace the proposed mechanism through events. Check alternatives such as selection, timing, different resources, measurement changes, or a common external cause.
5. Conclude supported, contradicted or unresolved within stated conditions. Choose the next missing observation or prospective test.

Use process tracing for sequences and mechanisms. Use realist evaluation to ask what works for whom and in which context. Borrow competing-hypothesis tables to organize diagnostic evidence, but do not turn inconsistency counts into probabilities or claim the table removes bias.

Historical replay tests a procedure on recorded cases; it does not rerun the original world. Modern models may already know historical outcomes, even with date-filtered retrieval. Label that limitation. Use fresh prospective forecasts to test transfer when practical.

A scenario calculates or explores consequences of assumptions. Keep its output separate from observed historical evidence. Simulated agents can suggest rival explanations; they do not create independent witnesses or users.
